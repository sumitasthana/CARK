Research progress · Learning and task unlearning

What our unlearning experiments show so far

We made a model’s task-3 accuracy fall to chance and stay there during one later lesson. We also found that averages can hide damage to individual tasks. Whether useful task-3 information remains inside the model is still unanswered.

01 · The purpose

Does low accuracy mean the model has lost the knowledge?

Our research asks what remains in a model after a task has been deliberately unlearned. A model may stop answering correctly, yet still contain information that could help it recover the task. We have not run that recovery test yet.

We first needed a model that could learn several tasks, a way to measure ordinary forgetting, and a controlled comparison that separated ordinary learning from deliberate unlearning. Those checks explain the experiments in this report.

TaskA group of ten image classes. Each task has its own numeric identifier, such as 3 or 15. The number is a label, not a difficulty score.
AccuracyThe percentage of validation images answered correctly. Each task has 500 validation images. Chance accuracy is 10% for ten classes.
HypernetworkA network that generates the weights of another network. In our experiments, it generates a task-specific ResNet50 image classifier.
UnlearningA deliberate update intended to remove a task’s useful behaviour. Ordinary forgetting is a score loss caused by learning something else.

In early attempts, the model either learned weakly or damaged other tasks when unlearning. We reviewed the initialization and training choices. Later runs used a documented Hyperfan-in initialization and a lower learning rate. The latest unlearning runner also corrected a difference between the weights used for its two loss terms. We did not isolate each change in a separate comparison, so we cannot credit one change for all later improvements.

Historical attempts used different settings and, in some cases, an earlier unlearning objective. Their detailed records remain in the experiment trajectory. The figures below use saved evidence from the later diagnostics.

02 · Before interpreting small effects

03 · Learning without deliberate unlearning

Protection greatly reduced ordinary forgetting in the batch

We tried five beta values while learning task 17. Every new trial started from the same model after tasks 3, 0, 9, and 5. This experiment contained no unlearning request. It asked whether changing protection could reduce damage caused by learning alone.

Without protection, the older-task average fell by 26.5 percentage points. Task 9 fell to 10.0%, and task 5 fell to 13.2%. With beta 0.1, the older-task average rose by 1.0 point and the largest individual drop was 0.4 points, while task 17 reached 50.8%.

The repeat did not reproduce that near-zero individual loss: its older-task average fell by 2.1 points and its largest drop was 5.6 points. We therefore kept beta 0.1 as a provisional setting, without calling it a proven optimum.

What we learned: older tasks can fail because of new learning alone. Protection helped substantially in these observations, but it did not guarantee exact retention.

An unchanged average hid losses on individual tasks

Tasks 3, 0, and 9 finished above their sequence-start scores. Task 17 fell after each new lesson. Learning task 14 also lowered task 1 from 56.8% to 49.0%, a 7.8-point loss during that lesson.

These observations do not support a simple rule that the earliest tasks suffer most. We also sampled the directions in which learning and protection changed the model. Some samples opposed each other, but those sparse measurements did not establish the cause of the score losses.

What we learned: report each task as well as the average. Improvement on one task can cancel a loss on another in the mean.

05 · The controlled unlearning comparison

Task 3 stayed at chance through one later lesson

Both branches loaded the same model saved after task 14. Branch A learned task 15. Branch B first unlearned task 3, then learned task 15. Old-task training images were not used. Old validation images were used only to measure scores.

A · Learning-only controlSame starting checkpoint → learn task 15.
B · Unlearning branchSame starting checkpoint → unlearn task 3 → learn task 15.

All eight pair checks passed: starting model, starting scores, CPU random state, GPU random state, initial task-15 code, image order, image values, and update count. Each branch completed 395 learning updates. The old task codes, chunk codes, and BatchNorm running values also remained unchanged.

Fixed settings: seed 0; beta 0.1; gamma 0.01; 100 unlearning updates; ten noise samples per update; learning rate 0.0001 for both operations; five learning epochs; batch size 64. The unlearning loss and protection loss both used actual generated model weights, including their scales and offsets. Earlier unlearning runs used a different noise representation.

The result we can report: the requested task lost accuracy and did not recover during this one later lesson. Chance accuracy does not show whether useful information remains in the model.

The retained-task average was close, but individual effects differed

Immediately after unlearning, the seven retained tasks had lost 1.54 points on average. At the end of task-15 learning, they finished 0.34 points lower on average in B than A. That small final average difference does not mean every retained task was unaffected.

Task 9 was 5.6 points lower in B, while task 5 was 9.6 points higher. The new task 15 reached 54.4% in A and 49.0% in B. This pair did not show improved new-task learning after unlearning.

Final scores also hid temporary damage

At the first unlearning update, task 17 fell from 50.0% to 28.2%, a temporary 21.8-point loss. Its score recovered to 50.0% by the end of unlearning. A final-only report would miss that disturbance.

The paper’s spill measure adds the absolute changes across retained tasks immediately after an unlearning request. Applying that definition here gives 10.8 percentage points, summed across seven tasks. This is different from both the 1.54-point average retained loss and the 0.34-point final difference between branches. Our measured relapse, the change in task-3 accuracy from after unlearning to the end of this short sequence, was 0.0 points. Paper, equations 4 and 5.

What we learned: functional forgetting can persist through later learning, while effects on retained tasks vary over time and across tasks.

06 · Interpreting the evidence

Our results partly agree with the paper

The authors report chance-level forget accuracy, low damage to retained tasks, resistance to later relapse, and improved learning in longer sequences. They also report a membership inference test of privacy. Paper, discussion and Tables 1–3.

ClaimOur assessmentReason
Requested task falls to chanceSupported in one requestTask 3 fell from 27.4% to 10.0%.

Other tasks remain stableMixed evidenceFinal mean losses were limited, but individual and temporary drops were larger.
Unlearning improves later learningNot supported in this pairTask 15 scored 5.4 points lower after unlearning. A longer saturation experiment was not performed.
Private or recoverable information is removedNot assessedNo membership inference or controlled recovery test was run.

These findings neither reproduce nor refute the paper as a whole. Our detailed pair uses one seed, moves task-3 unlearning to a later checkpoint, and uses beta 0.1 instead of the paper’s Tiny ImageNet beta 0.01. We use a constant learning rate of 0.0001; the paper states 0.001 with a scheduler. The full 30-request experiment, three-seed averages, and baseline comparisons have not been reproduced. Paper, implementation and Appendix C.

What this work has established

  1. Ordinary learning can lower older-task scores. A learning-only control is necessary when measuring unlearning damage.
  2. Weight protection helped substantially in the beta trials, but one result cannot establish a stable optimum.
  3. Individual task scores and intermediate measurements provide information that averages and final scores miss.
  4. In one matched pair, task-3 accuracy fell to chance and remained there through one subsequent lesson.

The open research question: can useful task-3 information be recovered from the saved unlearned model with a small, fixed adaptation budget? A suitable control must distinguish residual task-3 knowledge from ordinary learning and transfer from other tasks. This experiment has not been run. Further experiments are paused for reflection.
